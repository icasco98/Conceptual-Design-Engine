What the next batch should work on: not more rules — the automatic search. It never once produced a plan with zero problems, in any of the 56 test cases, before or after any of this. The reason is mechanical and fixable: the test plans start with every room sitting 0.3 m apart, and the search nudges rooms around at random, so it almost never lands two rooms exactly flush against each other — which is the only way a door can exist between them. It needs a move that snaps a room to its neighbour's wall. That is one new kind of move in the search, not a new rule.

What this is, in one page

This tool has two halves. One is the drawing editor you use by hand. The other — the half this report is about — is a set of rules: things the tool knows about how houses work, which it uses both to tell you what is wrong with a plan you have drawn, and to judge plans that its own automatic search invents.

This batch added ten rules to that set. Every one of them comes from a real, established convention or building-code requirement, and each one is written up in the code with the reason it exists, so the next person can argue with it rather than guess at it. Nothing here changes the drawing editor: these rules only ever look and report. They never move, resize or connect anything on their own.

The tool separates two kinds of finding, and this report keeps that distinction:

  • A problem is the tool saying the plan does not work — a bedroom with no way out, a door that cannot physically be built.
  • A suggestion is the tool saying the plan works but costs more or is worse to live in than it needs to be — a kitchen too far from the car, pipes that could have shared one stack.

Enough satisfied suggestions can never outweigh a single problem. That ordering is deliberate and has not changed.

How to read the pictures

Each diagram is a plan view, drawn to scale, with the plot boundary as the dashed rectangle. Green bars are interior doors. Orange bars are doors to the outside. Rooms outlined in red sit outside the plot boundary. These are drawn by the tool itself, from the same numbers the rules read, so a door you can see here is a door the rules can see too.

Pass 1

Two rules about where rooms sit relative to each other.

ACCEPTEDThe kitchen should be a short walk from the garage

Standard residential planning: the service-entry sequence, garage → mudroom → kitchen.

Shopping is unloaded from the car and carried to the kitchen. It is the one trip in a house you make over and over with both hands full, and if the kitchen is at the far end of the plan you make it the long way every week for as long as you live there.

The tool already knew the garage should be near the mudroom and near the entrance. It had never been told what that route is for. It now checks that the kitchen is at most two doors from the garage — which garage → mudroom → kitchen satisfies exactly.

It deliberately does not ask for a door straight from the garage into the kitchen. The mudroom is there on purpose, to keep the garage's dirt, noise and exhaust fumes out of the room where food is prepared. This is a suggestion, not a fault: a house whose kitchen is far from the garage is inconvenient, not broken.

ACCEPTEDA WC must not open directly onto a kitchen or dining room

Residential building regulation (England & Wales Approved Document G states it outright) and much older general practice.

A room with a toilet in it gets a separating space — a small lobby, a hall, a second door — before a room where food is prepared or eaten. Opening the WC should not open it onto the food, and nobody should sit at a dining table looking at a WC door.

The important half of this rule is what it does not say. It has no objection to a WC being next to a kitchen. Sharing a wall is normal and often deliberate, because it puts both rooms' plumbing on a single stack and saves money. It is specifically the doorway that is the defect, so the tool looks at where the doors are, not at which rooms touch.

This counts as a real problem, not a suggestion. Nothing the tool already had could catch it: its privacy rules skip bathrooms on purpose, because a bathroom is allowed to open onto either a public or a private room.

Did anything else get worse?

Nothing regressed. All 56 scenarios scored exactly what they scored before, on both rules.

Pass 2

Two rules about whether what is drawn could actually be built.

ACCEPTEDA door needs enough shared wall to physically exist

A common interior door leaf is 762 mm; codes put a required exit door's clear opening at 813 mm (IRC R311.2).

Two rooms can clip past each other at a corner, sharing a few centimetres of wall, and the tool would happily draw a door across that sliver — and then treat those two rooms as connected for every other purpose. Below about 0.9 m of shared wall there is no doorway, whatever the drawing says.

This matters more than it sounds, because of a promise the tool makes elsewhere: when it shows you a route through the house, it is asserting you can actually walk it. That is why two rooms sharing a wall with no door in it get no connection at all — not a faint one. A door on 12 cm of wall is that same false claim arriving from the other direction, so it is treated as a real problem.

It is scored by how far short the wall falls. A wall 5 cm short is something a designer fixes by nudging one wall; a wall 5 cm long is a fiction. Both are real, and they are not the same size of real.

ACCEPTEDEvery room with a bed in it needs a wall facing outside

The emergency escape and rescue opening required in every sleeping room (IRC R310, and near-universal elsewhere).

Every bedroom needs a window or door big enough to climb out of and for a firefighter to climb in through. A room with no outside wall at all cannot have one. A landlocked bedroom is not an awkward bedroom — it is not a bedroom.

The tool measures outside-facing wall, not windows, and says so plainly: it has no window objects, and inventing some to answer this would be inventing the answer. What it can say for certain is that a room with no wall facing outside can have no window at all.

REVERTEDThe same rule applied to every habitable room, not just bedrooms

Natural light and ventilation for habitable rooms (IRC R303.1).

This was the first version of the rule above, and it was pulled. It flagged the tool's own demonstration house — correctly, in that the dining room there genuinely has no outside wall, and wrongly, in that the code has an explicit and very commonly used exception: an inside room may take its light and air from an adjoining room through a wide enough opening. That is exactly how a dining room open to a living room is normally justified.

This tool models solid walls and doors, and nothing in between. It cannot tell a wide open archway from masonry, so it would have flagged a completely ordinary arrangement as a defect. The escape-window rule has no such exception — a bedroom cannot borrow an escape route from the room next door — so the narrower version is the one this tool can honestly stand behind.

Reverted for the right reason and not to make a test pass: the demonstration house's dining room stops being reported because the rule no longer makes any claim about dining rooms, not because the check was weakened.

Did anything else get worse?

Pass 3

Two rules about whether a room is pleasant and usable, rather than whether it works.

ACCEPTEDA room people sit in wants two sides facing out, not one

Dual-aspect design; discouragement of single-aspect dwellings is written into residential design guidance, and is far older as practice in hot climates.

Air only moves through a room if it has somewhere to come in and somewhere to go out. Windows on one side give a room daylight and nothing else: it takes the afternoon sun, holds the heat, and has no way to flush it out overnight. A second side — opposite for a real through draught, or round a corner for a weaker one — is what lets a room cool itself.

In this climate that is the difference between a bedroom that is usable in summer without running the air conditioning all night and one that is not. It is recorded as a suggestion rather than a fault, on the same principle as everything else in that category: the room works, it is just more expensive to live in.

ACCEPTEDA room stretched past three times longer than it is wide

A planning heuristic, and flagged as such — the standard literature discusses room proportion in the 1:1 to 1:2 range and treats anything past roughly 1:2.5 as awkward.

Past about 1:3, a room stops being a room you can arrange furniture in and becomes a corridor with a use attached: the bed or the table has to sit at one end and the rest is left over.

This is the one rule in the batch that is a rule of thumb rather than a code requirement, and it is labelled that way in the code itself, using the same wording the project already uses for its other reasoned-but-uncited rules. The threshold is set at 1:3 deliberately — past the point where anyone would argue, rather than in the middle of the range.

It is also the only rule in the batch that gives the automatic search something it can steadily improve. The search resizes rooms constantly, and until now nothing priced the shape it left them in.

One existing test needed its fixture resized, and it is worth saying why that is not a test being bent to fit. That test used a 30 m × 4 m living room, sized that way purely to dodge an unrelated threshold — at 7.5:1 it is now also an over-long room, correctly. What the test asserts is unchanged; only the dimensions moved.

Did anything else get worse?

Nothing regressed. All 56 scenarios unchanged — both rules are suggestions, so neither can raise a scenario's problem count.

Pass 4

Two rules about construction: what pipes cost, and what fits in a wall.

ACCEPTEDAn upstairs wet room should sit over a wet room below

Plumbing stack sharing — one of the oldest cost rules in residential construction.

Bathrooms, kitchens and laundries all need water in, waste out and a vent up. Stack them and those pipes run together in one service void. Scatter them and each one needs its own boxed-in stack, dropped through whatever room happens to be underneath and boxed in there too.

It is a maintenance rule as much as a cost one: a stack you can reach in one place is a stack you can fix in one place. Recorded as a suggestion — a house with scattered bathrooms works perfectly well and simply costs more to build.

Ground-floor wet rooms are never flagged: there is nothing below them, and a single-storey house should not be told its plumbing is badly arranged.

ACCEPTEDTwo doorways cannot be cut into the same stretch of wall

Door width and clearance — the most basic dimensional discipline in laying out a room.

Two openings less than a door's width apart in one wall are not two doors. Their frames overlap and the leaves foul each other, so one of them has to move before either can be built or used.

The tool restricts this to two doors in the same wall. Two doors 0.6 m apart around a corner also interfere in practice, but how badly depends on which way each door is hung and which way it swings — and this tool records where a doorway is, not which side its hinges are on. Rather than guess a hinge, it reports only the case that is true however the doors are hung.

Did anything else get worse?

Nothing regressed. All 56 scenarios unchanged. The wet-room rule could not possibly move them — every scenario in the suite is single-storey.

Pass 5

Two rules about receiving guests — the part of this brief the tool understood least.

ACCEPTEDA diwaniya is entered from the street, not through the house

The institution itself — and a convention this project had already written down in its own notes without ever checking it.

A diwaniya receives visitors who have no relationship with the family, at hours the family is not entertaining. The entire point of the room is that a guest gets in and out without ever crossing the household's private life. That only works if the room has its own door onto the street.

The project's own notes said exactly this — that the diwaniya's street door is what keeps a guest's movement out of the household's, and that the room type only marks the destination. That was true in an unhelpful way: the type marked the destination and nothing ever verified the door.

This is a real fault, not a matter of degree. A diwaniya you reach through the family's hallway is not a slightly compromised diwaniya; it is a second sitting room, and the household has lost the separation it built the room for. None of the tool's existing checks could see it: the room is reachable, its doors skip no privacy step, and the existing diwaniya rules only talk about shared walls.

It is set as a flag on the room type rather than a hard-coded check for one room name, so a project with no diwaniya has nothing flagged, and a project whose guest house or home surgery wants the same treatment gets it by setting one field.

This is the one rule in the whole batch that changed the recorded numbers — see the regression note below.

ACCEPTEDA guest-receiving room should have a guest WC near it

The guest WC: a visitor should not need the family's own bathroom.

A room built for receiving people who are not family needs a WC those people can use without walking into the household's private wing. Without one, the separation the diwaniya or reception room exists to create holds right up until a visitor needs the bathroom, and then it does not.

It asks specifically for a powder room, not a full family bathroom: a family bathroom near the diwaniya is not the same provision, it is the family's bathroom with guests in it. And it asks for it within two doors, not directly off the room — a small lobby in between is the normal and better arrangement.

One honest limit, worth stating: the tool can only say the powder room you have is in the wrong place. It cannot say you should have one, because it never penalises a house for lacking a room type entirely — the same reason it never criticises a house for having no garage.

Proven by a hand-built example only. None of the tool's five stress-test household programs includes a powder room, so these rules are silent across the whole suite. The next batch could reasonably add one to the programs.

Did anything else get worse?

The diwaniya rule moved the recorded baseline, and this is the one place in the batch where a number went up. 28 of the 56 scenarios gained exactly 1 problem, and they are exactly the 28 that contain a diwaniya — the Extended Gulf and Multigenerational programs, on all 14 plot shapes. The scenario builder gives every plan one exterior door, on the entrance, and nothing else, so every one of those 28 plans really does have a diwaniya you can only reach through the family's front door. The number went up because the tool can now see a fault that was always there. The other 28 scenarios did not move at all, and no scenario moved by anything other than that +1. The frozen record was rewritten to match, deliberately and with the reason recorded — the wrong move would have been to quietly give the scenario builder a diwaniya door, which is editing the evidence rather than fixing the plan.

What the numbers actually say

The honest headline of this batch is not the ten rules. It is this: across all 56 test scenarios, before and after everything above, the search has never once produced a plan with zero problems. That was already true when this batch started and it is still true now, and no amount of tuning moved it.

That is worth being blunt about, because five rounds of re-tuning could easily be presented as five small wins. They were not. Across six training runs the average problem count moved by well under one percent each time, and the clean rate stayed at zero throughout. The tuning is not broken — it is correctly reporting that there is almost nothing there to win.

The reason is specific and fixable. Every test scenario starts as rooms laid out in simple rows with a fixed 0.3 m gap between each one. The search improves a plan by nudging rooms — a little to the left, a little bigger, a few degrees round — and keeping whatever scores better. But for two rooms to be connected, their walls have to meet within 4 cm. A search that moves rooms by random amounts essentially never hits a 4 cm target, so rooms almost never end up touching, so doors almost never become possible, so nearly every room in nearly every scenario ends up unreachable. That single mechanical fact accounts for the overwhelming majority of the problem count in every scenario in the suite.

No setting of step size, temperature or move preference fixes that, which is exactly what six training runs found. What fixes it is a new kind of move: one that slides a room until it is flush against a neighbour's wall, instead of hoping to land there. That is a change to the search's repertoire rather than its dials, and it is the single most valuable thing the next batch could do.

Nine of the ten new rules found nothing at all in these 56 scenarios — almost all of them are about doors, and these scenarios have practically no doors for the rules to examine. What is guarding this from here on

Two things now exist that did not before. Every scenario's problem count is frozen in a file under version control and checked automatically on every test run, so a future change that quietly makes a plan worse fails immediately instead of showing up as a number in a table nobody diffs. And every search-tuning run is logged — including the ones that lost — so the next person can tell a direction that was tried and rejected from one nobody has tried.

Honest limits of this batch
  • Six of the ten rules are proven by hand-built examples only. They are correct and tested, but the 56 stress-test scenarios contain nothing for them to find — no interior doors to speak of, no second storey, no powder rooms. That is a gap in the scenarios, not in the rules.
  • The tool measures walls, not windows. It has no window objects. It can be certain that a room with no outside wall has no window; it cannot check a window that could exist but was never drawn.
  • The tool does not know which way a door swings. It records where a doorway is, not which side its hinges are on, which is why the door-clearance rule stops at two doors in one wall rather than guessing about doors around a corner.
  • One rule is a rule of thumb, and is labelled so. Room proportion at 1:3 is a planning convention, not a code requirement, and the threshold was set past the point of argument rather than in the middle of the range.
